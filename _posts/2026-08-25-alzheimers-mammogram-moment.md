*Blood biomarkers are transforming Alzheimer's assessment. Their impact depends on accessibility, coverage, and continued investment in basic science.*

When my father started changing at around age 67, my family did not immediately think of illness. He seemed emotionally more fragile, and familiar tasks became more difficult for him. We initially interpreted those changes as aging or personality. Only later did we understand that something was failing in his brain. Even then, my family never received a clear neurological diagnosis. We did not know which disease caused his decline.

That experience occurred years before I became a neuroscientist. It ultimately redirected my life. I left my legal career and pursued a PhD in neuroscience focusing on the neurobiology of Alzheimer's disease.

Today, I view my family's experience differently because diagnostic tools are changing quickly. Alzheimer's disease may soon be detectable early enough for earlier intervention, much as routine mammography allows breast cancer to be detected at an earlier stage.

## A blood test is changing Alzheimer's assessment

In May 2025, the FDA cleared the first blood test to aid Alzheimer's disease diagnosis. The Lumipulse test measures plasma phosphorylated tau 217, or p-tau217, and amyloid-beta 1-42. It uses their ratio to estimate whether amyloid plaques are present in the brain. The FDA cleared the test for adults aged 55 years or older who have signs and symptoms of cognitive decline [1].

Clinical confirmation of Alzheimer's pathology has often relied on amyloid PET imaging or cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers. PET imaging is costly and requires specialized facilities, and cerebrospinal fluid collection requires a lumbar puncture. A standard blood draw could make biomarker assessment substantially easier, though it remains a proxy measurement and is less certain than either of those confirmatory tests.

The FDA validation study found that 91.7% of positive results agreed with amyloid-positive PET or cerebrospinal fluid findings. It also found that 97.3% of negative results agreed with amyloid-negative findings. Fewer than 20% of results were indeterminate and required further evaluation [1].

Separate evidence suggests that blood biomarkers may also improve decisions in primary care. At the Alzheimer's Association International Conference 2026, researchers reported results from a large real-world study in Sweden. Primary care physicians reached 93% diagnostic accuracy after reviewing blood-test results, nearing the accuracy of dementia specialists evaluating the same patients [2].

## Earlier assessment is critical

Alzheimer's disease is known to begin its underlying pathology decades before cognitive symptoms appear [3]. Earlier assessment is particularly relevant because the FDA-approved drugs lecanemab and donanemab slowed clinical decline in trials of patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's disease [4,5]. Neither has been shown to help once the disease has progressed further.

Blood testing cannot replace a complete clinical evaluation. It could, however, make early assessment more accessible, especially where specialist care is limited.

Living in Lubbock has made this challenge tangible to me. Lubbock serves as a medical hub for a large region of West Texas, yet neurology appointments can still involve substantial waits. Patients from smaller surrounding communities may face longer drives to reach specialist care. Blood biomarkers could eventually help primary care clinicians determine which patients need further specialist evaluation and which need investigation for other causes of decline.

Current FDA clearance, however, applies to symptomatic patients only. It does not authorize routine screening of cognitively healthy adults [1]. Medicare also does not currently provide a specific coverage pathway for blood-based dementia screening [6,7].

Congress is considering the bipartisan Alzheimer's Screening and Prevention Act, or ASAP Act (H.R. 6130 and S. 3267). Rather than supporting one specific commercial test, the legislation would create Medicare coverage authority for eligible early-detection screening tests beginning in 2028 [6,7].

The Alzheimer's Impact Movement calls this the field's "mammogram moment" [8]. Mammography existed long before Congress created routine Medicare coverage in 1991. In the years that followed, screening became far more widespread and breast cancer mortality declined substantially [8].

Mammography itself did not cure breast cancer. Its public-health impact came from enabling earlier, more accessible detection and connecting abnormal results to appropriate follow-up care.

## What would a true mammogram moment require?

We have not reached that point yet, and getting there starts further upstream than coverage policy. Every blood biomarker and treatment rests on decades of basic neuroscience research. Scientists first had to define disease biology, develop ways to measure biomarkers, and validate those measures in large clinical studies.

Much of that work has depended on sustained public investment in biomedical research. My own PhD work examined how amyloid and tau pathologies interact in Alzheimer's disease mouse models. I focused on whether molecular changes observed in experimental models align with patterns seen in human disease. Research like mine does not directly produce a blood test, but it contributes to the biological foundation on which diagnostic technologies are built.

Sustained NIH investment remains essential for refining biomarkers, validating them across diverse populations, and developing the next generation of diagnostics and treatments. But scientific evidence alone does not guarantee access. Clinicians need guidance on when to use these tests, patients with uncertain results need access to follow-up care, and insurance policy must keep pace with the evidence.

Blood tests already exist for assessing symptomatic patients. The next challenge is determining how far their benefits can extend. The ASAP Act addresses one part of that question by creating a potential Medicare pathway for eligible early-detection screening tests. Continued research addresses another by establishing when those tests are accurate, useful, and clinically meaningful.

Discovery opened the door. Access will decide who walks through it.
